# About this App

As researchers in the interpretative social sciences and humanities, there is more to read than ever. (It has ever been thus!) While Anthropologists and historians remain committed to the practice of long term, deep engagement with the particularities of the cultural lifeworlds and historical and contemporary contexts we write about, through which deep, long term, ethnographic fieldwork and archival research are crucial. Yet, over the over the last few decades, the amount of ethnographic or historical material that a research could accumulate just in their own work has grown exponentially. Thousands of photographs and notes from fieldworks, along with audio and video recording. Its not abnormal for historians to accumulate thousands of photographs of historical documents, along with hundreds of primary and secondary sources. Some has been digitized by others, much has never been digitalized. But, the work of finding out what to read remains daunting, even if one were to limit oneself to the genres of work that we work on, which is notebooks, personal archives, library materials, historical documents, account books, legal archives, maps, property records, and notarial records. While anthropologists remain committed to telling stories with theoretical purpose, and historians remain committed to advancing the historiography of their particulars areas of research. Tools to keep up with, work with, and make sense of this material are hard to come by.

There is much that we can and should learn from tool, techniques, and approaches that have emerged over the last few years in different fields. Digital humanities scholars have put forth powerful tools for making sense of written text and doing Natural Language Processing (NLP). Digital humanities scholars have developed tools to process and visualize, map, connect, and make sense and find meaning within large corpuses of textual data. Manuscript scholars have developed careful and considered tools and techniques to work with small and large archives to make high quality, careful, digital renditions, transcriptions, and editions of historical texts, using machine learning techniques and Automatic Text Recognition, some in well served languages like English in Latin script, and others in often under resourced languages and scripts, some of which have no machine learning tools the level of semantic meaning and density found on a page can be quite different. At the same time, the rapid advancement of Large Language Models at both the expensive frontier, as well as far cheaper, smaller, more specialized, open weight models. That is, it is possible to near perforce or sometimes very low Character Error Rates for automatically converted handwritten and printed scripts in many languages: using local and cloud models, private frontier models with dubious economics and environmental costs, as well as open-weight, specialized, smaller, vision language models that can do the same work, more reliably, predictably, and far cheaper, with proper tooling. All of these fields have show there are powerful, adaptable ways to do useful work. From small, efficient, open-weight machine learning (HTR) and open-weight LLM models, to tools to segment a page, to create structured data out of unstructured sources, for example pulling out names ofd people, places, and things, but also of concepts nad arguments, of statements with attribution, if its available, and at times, gender, age, and other material. It becomes possible, epseicalyl when combined with local or public gazetteers, open source maps, and authoritative lists of canonical names, to make reasonable predictions and extrapolations from source materials. That is, at the scale of a dataset, Artificial intelligence, Machine Learning (AI), Natural Language Processing, Natural Language Graphs, is proving itself incredibly useful. But, anthropologists, historians, journalists, storytellers, genealogists, and many others people are interested in making datasets, but in telling stories with theoretical purpose, in writing micro-histories, and in making sense, in the sense of doing the craft work of developing understanding through deep engagement with texts. 

It is in this world, that AI is sweeping through the academy, and many find ways to get AI to do *their* work for them, producing slop on along the way, and often mistaking deep understanding. The reaction here is to turn away from AI. But, to do that is to mist the fact that there are many ways these tools are quite useful. I believe using AI to write a thesis or dissertation or book for you is a dubious endeavour, just as asking it to interpret the past. What’s the point of that. But, if it might soon be possible to have as common place accurate Automatic Transcription of entire holdings of handwritten material, as the Swedish National Library now does, the questions becomes how do you *make of such materials, and how might you use the same rigorous, cost effective techniques for far smaller projects.* That is, however can you do what scholars with important, specialized techniques, been doing for years, for much smaller projects. Fichero emerges for my conviction that one of the issues is one of tooling. 

Many tools for working with archival or research materials, take one of two approaches. Either they’re incredibly powerful, but quite difficulty to use. Mastering such tools becomes something only specialists (be that in Automatic Text Recognition, Computer Vision) are able to do. That is, those with the affinity, openness, expertise, and ability to learn to use the terminal to use these tools. Or, they abstract away all such complexity, rigour, and power, into chatbot style interfaces which are often both sycophantic (they tell you what they thing you want to hear) and prone to hallucination (they predict the logical next token that may at times seem perfect and at others have little relationship to reality.) 

In the first instance, the idea of launching a CLI, setting up an environment, installing Python dependencies, and running scripts can seem overwhelming to many in the interpretative social sciences and humanities. I am not convinced that the only path forward is to push all scholars to becoming computer programers, just as I don’t think all those working in digital humanities out to take up two years and do some actual mining (as I did for my doctoral fiedlwork) before they engage in data mining. But, at the same time, the difficulty in tooling—and here I mean that so often steps to get the machine to do what yo are trying to get it to do become the point in and of itself, rather than reading and engaging with the datasets in ways (be that deep reading in the vein of Mary Ann Wolf, or distant reading in the vein of XXX.) In Proust and the Squid, Mary Ann Wolf and the Reader over our Shoulder makes the point that reading itself rewrites tour brain. While those in the interpretative social sciences and humanities might have a natural affinity to such perspectives. Others in the digital humanities are doing incredibly interesting work on distant reading, where they are able to find patterns at the scale of entire genres of literature, in ways that are made possible by being able to process hundreds of thousand of digitized books. 

Instead of this though, in many instances, students and others turn to chatbots and other tools to try to do their work. That is, here the liable to seem like magic, with  results that are often inaccurate, with mistakes, hallucinations, and methodological difficulties.

I’m not convinced however the only solutions are tools that require a level of comitment to exactitude, and precision, in entering in the terminal where adding a `.` cna change the meaning of of a script radically, to the abandonment of tools and thought to large language models. Instead, I am convinced that there is a way forward where the issue is one of tooling. That is, the tools could be easier to use, more human, and more useful.

In a sense, here Fichero as an apps aims to inhabit a space somewhere in between. It is an app. It doesn’t require the command line, although it relies on the same community of open source tools and approaches that digital librarians, digital humanities scholars, and those working at using HTR to understand the past. On the other hand, it does not abstract away all thought, mistaking the uploading of materials to an agent or a chat bot on a hosted or run by a cloud service as good scholarship.

Fichero’s focus, instead, is to make AI workflows accessible in an app where much *less* technical proficiency is required. The app lets you build a project, add sources, navigate and organize them, automatically make meta data about them, make connections and write about sources, and read your research collection on your own computers. It allows you to iteratively experiment with different approaches to using approaches from digital humanities to do useful things. For example, the Automatic Text Recognition, of a handwritten document, cheaply, and on your own computer, can offer a first-draft transcription, or a way to extract useful. Maybe an index of people, places, and dates, as they appear in a document, or to make a knowledge graph which consists of simple statements, extracted from a page, in ways that make it easier to navigate, understand, and make sense of metadata. Thus, Fichero offers a place to both read and make sense of your research materials, while at the same time aim to make your more familiar with some of these techniques.

Fichero is in a sense not unlike an academic workspace, with a place for not only hundreds or thousands or tens of thousand of primary sources (e.g. archival materials, scans of documents, newspapers, audio recordings, photographs, etc.), but also secondary sources (e.g. digitized books, academic articles, newspapers, field notes, etc.), but also a place to come back to such sources, to curate them, to organize the ones you care about, and to take notes about them, to organize and collect such materials, and later to come to search the materials. To this, it adds tools to automatically transcribe text and other sources, to build an index of people, places, dates, etc., to show connections between such entities, think about materials, and to look through them.
