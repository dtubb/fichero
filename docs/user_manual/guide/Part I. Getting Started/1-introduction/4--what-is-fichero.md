# What is Fichero 

The word Fichero is literally from the Spanish for a card-file cabinet: the index-card filing systems researchers and scholars have long used (e.g. Walter Benjamin's *Arcades Project*, Niklas Luhmann's Zettelkasten, the physical card catalogues of archives and libraries, or the field notes ethnographers and archival notes historians have always kept). Such boxes of index cards are tools for accumulating and organizing knowledge.

Fichero was built to make available to researchers a place to organize their images, historical documents, and materials alongside these sorts of index cards (that is, extracted data, transcripts, metadata), to find things through a search function, and to organize and navigate the index cards. Fichero aims to help anyone, but particularly academics, historians, anthropologists, journalists, and others, to make sense of unstructured materials. In Fichero, the application is fast. Data extracted is tied to the source image. Click on an extracted person, and you will see what Fichero has learned about that person, with links to the source text. Read a claim you want to see the source for, and it will take you to the original source.

Every researcher sees their research library differently; as such, Fichero is a flexible app, which adapts to how you want to research, rather than forcing you to adapt to one particular way of working. It works as well to help you use sometimes expensive, powerful frontier vision language models to work with hard-to-read historical documents from 15th- or 16th-century hands, as to use free models built into your computer to transcribe modern typescripts and manuscripts easily. You can use Fichero to try to find connections between dozens of PDFs, or hundreds of thousands of images. Fichero has many built-in tools and workflows, which you can chain together to do useful things. But it is flexible enough for you so that you can do whatever you want.

The core concepts of Fichero are these:

	•	Its interface should be familiar enough to anyone who has used a Mac.
	
	•	You can work with any number of images or documents, quickly.
	
	•	You can use and compare different models and providers, and experiment with different tools.
	
	•	You can edit extracted data. For example, merge different spellings of the same person together, or delete extracted "facts" that are misinterpretations.
	
	•	Each piece of extracted data is always linked back to its source.
	
	•	You can navigate and explore your documents using different tools and workflows.
	
	•	You can collect together documents, make annotations, keep notes, and try to understand a document.
	
	•	A Fichero library is essentially a digital place to store images, documents, web pages, recorded interviews, and other research, and explore it using AI tools and techniques.
	
	•	You can have more than one Fichero window, or tab, open at once to refer to different documents in different libraries.

The underlying philosophy of Fichero emerges from an archival project in the Colombian Pacific, and a team of young people working on writing catalogue entries for digitized historical material. The tools they were using (tablets, computers, and a word processor) have their place, but they were tools that got in the way of trying to work with an archive. To navigate it and search it. To read it, correct transcriptions, and search them again. Fichero aims to give you easier ways to read, take notes, search, and correct errors in the transcription and extracted data.

But, in the end, the point of Fichero is to give you a place to keep materials, to jot ideas, to take notes, to make connections, and to do the slow hard work of making sense. Fichero gives you a place to work with research material, a place that lets you experiment with different tools, and which can accommodate different ways of working.
