Chapter One
Gold and the Household Economy

Guapi, a medium-sized dog with short, spotted black-and-white hair, stood at the edge of the sluggish water and scratched and whined. Did he want to give chase? Two rows of men fanned out on both sides of the slow-moving creek that fed the river a hundred yards downstream. Some stood waist-deep in the water; others bobbed in the current, circling their arms wide to keep their heads dry.

A row of white dots, the spine of a paca, broke the dark water and then disappeared between the rows of men. The lowland paca is a giant rodent, the size of a small dog. Its legs are short, its body chubby, and its back distinctive, with rows of white dots. The paca resurfaced a few meters downstream and dove deep again.

The men in the water heaved forward, and Guapi threw himself after them in frantic chase, but in the water, the dog was at a disadvantage. The paca surfaced for air where the creek joined the river, having escaped between the dog and the lines of hunters. Pedro, wearing a hat made of reeds, left one of the lines of hunters and swung himself onto a long wooden canoe with two other men already in it.

More so than Pedro, Don Alfonso habitually entered into the wider cash economy by selling gold. Along with mining and his gardens, Don Alfonso had other sources of income.

Don Alfonso would fund his trips to Medellín or Pereira from the sale of gold from his mine, calling these shopping trips his rebusque. He lived through the rebusque. Unlike Pedro, who seldom left the village, Don Alfonso had gone over the Andes to the Eje Cafetero to look for work — picking coffee or engaging in petty trading.

While Don Alfonso worked artisanally, his technique was different from Martina and Pedro's. For one thing, he did not dig, because he was too old and too tired. Instead, he let the water wash down a trench in the jungle from a reservoir that an excavator had carved into the landscape above his mine three years before.

The water ran into a small sluice box — a flat-bottomed wooden trough with riffles to catch the gold — and then into a settling pond at the bottom of the hill. The water turned milky-white with fine silt. Don Alfonso sold the gold he collected to enter the cash economy, a more reliable path than farming alone.

More so than Pedro, Don Alfonso relied on a dual household economy of subsistence production and mining. He maintained kitchen gardens alongside his mining operations, providing food for the household while the mine provided cash.

He called these shopping trips his rebusque3 voices › — the word carrying connotations of resourceful improvisation, a making-do that is both practical and culturally valued in the Chocó. He lived through the rebusque.

40

Ever since he had been a young man and gone to Saudi Arabia, Don Alfonso had understood the value of movement. The city was not foreign to him; it was a resource.

He traveled by bus from Quibdó to Medellín, a journey of many hours through the mountains, carrying a worn leather bag and a small notebook in which he wrote the names of suppliers and the prices he remembered from last time. He had a regular contact among the merchants in Medellín — a man who gave him a good price on machetes without asking too many questions.

He compared prices at several wholesale shops before buying, walking unhurried from stall to stall, drinking coffee, asking after family members. He knew which wholesale vendors to trust and which to avoid. He mistrusted credit arrangements with merchants, preferring always to pay in gold dust or cash.

He counted his gold dust at a jewelry shop in the centro, weighing it on a small scale the jeweler kept behind the counter. The price that day was good. He stayed in a cheap hostal near the central market, sharing a room with two other men from the Chocó he had met on the bus.

He considered the rebusque a skill worth teaching — not a survival strategy born of desperation but a practiced competence, learned over decades. He took pride in his self-sufficiency and knowledge of the city.
