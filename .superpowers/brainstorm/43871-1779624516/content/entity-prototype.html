Gold and the Household Economy

A few weeks before Christmas 2011, Don Alfonso, Pedro's spry elderly neighbor, and I went to work his family mine. While Don Alfonso worked artisanally, his technique was different from Martina and Pedro's.

For one thing, he did not dig, because he was too old and too tired. Instead, he let the water wash down a trench in the jungle from a reservoir that an excavator had carved into the landscape above his mine three years before.

More so than Pedro, Don Alfonso habitually entered into the wider cash economy by selling gold. Along with mining and his gardens, Don Alfonso had other sources of income.

Don Alfonso would fund his trips to Medellín or Pereira from the sale of gold from his mine, calling these shopping trips his rebusque.

He lived through the rebusque. Unlike Pedro, who seldom left the village, Don Alfonso had gone over the Andes to the Eje Cafetero to look for work — picking coffee or engaging in petty trading.

Don Alfonso gone over the Andes to the Eje Cafetero to look for work — picking coffee or engaging in petty trading; went to Saudi Arabia as a young man; traveled by bus from Quibdó to Medellín; called his daughter-in-law from Medellín; rented an apartment in Medellín; shopped bulk packages of inexpensive merchandise; compared prices at several wholesale shops before buying; bought machetes and household tools for resale; did not want to spend much money on a bad lunch (mundongo); bought food that day and on the trip back to Quibdó; had a regular contact among merchants in Medellín; was recognized by a shopkeeper from a previous visit; knew which wholesale vendors to trust; mistrusted credit arrangements with merchants; counted his gold dust at a jewelry shop in the centro; stayed in a cheap hostal near the central market; carried merchandise on the bus back to Quibdó; resold goods in his village at a small profit; distributed some goods to neighbors on return; brought news from the city back to the community; considered the rebusque a skill worth teaching younger men; took pride in his self-sufficiency and knowledge of the city; compared Pereira favorably with Medellín for certain goods; kept careful mental accounts of his expenditures; worked coffee in the Eje Cafetero; pushed bags at a market depot; rested in his hammock after the journey; described himself as someone who knew how to move between worlds; described the bus station in Medellín as chaotic but manageable. — Tubb (2020), Shifting Livelihoods, Ch. 2, pp. 4–16
