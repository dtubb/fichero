I. Don Alfonso — Person

Don Alfonso is an artisanal gold miner and itinerant trader from a village in the Colombian Pacific lowlands. His livelihood rested on a dual household economy of subsistence production and mining, which distinguished him markedly from his neighbour Pedro.1 More so than Pedro, he habitually entered into the wider cash economy2 by selling the gold extracted from his mine.

Don Alfonso funded his trips to Medellín and Pereira from the sale of gold from his mine,3 calling these commercial expeditions his rebusque.4 The word — from the verb rebuscar, to forage or rummage — carried connotations of resourceful self-reliance that Don Alfonso treated as a transmissible skill requiring city knowledge and trusted merchant contacts.5

Over the course of his adult life, Don Alfonso gone over the Andes to the Eje Cafetero to look for work — picking coffee or engaging in petty trading.6 He also went to Saudi Arabia as a young man,7 one of the older Chocoano migrants to the Gulf before the wave of the 2000s. These movements constituted a geography of labour that extended well beyond the Pacific lowlands.

On his commercial trips to Medellín, Don Alfonso exhibited a careful economic rationality: he compared prices at several wholesale shops before buying,8 knew which wholesale vendors to trust,9 and mistrusted credit arrangements with merchants.10 Before departure he counted his gold dust at a jewelry shop in the centro11 and stayed in a cheap hostal near the central market.12

On his return journeys, Don Alfonso carried merchandise on the bus back to Quibdó13 and subsequently resold goods in his village at a small profit.14 He brought news from the city back to the community — prices, politics, who had been seen — functioning as an informal information broker as much as a commercial one.15

Don Alfonso considered the rebusque a skill worth teaching and took pride in his self-sufficiency and knowledge of the city.16 He spoke of the city with a proprietorial familiarity, as if it were a space he had made his own through years of repeated negotiation.

II. Pedro — Person

Pedro represents the more rooted pole of the livelihood spectrum in the same village. Unlike Don Alfonso, he seldom left the village17 and relied almost entirely on subsistence farming and garden produce.18 His world was organised around the village and the forest rather than the wider cash economy.

Pedro had a smaller artisanal mine than Don Alfonso19 and, when cash was tight, depended on kin networks for cash inputs during lean months20 rather than commercial circuits. He maintained an extensive household garden with plantains and corn21 and bought tools locally through the village store rather than Medellín wholesalers.22

Taken together, Don Alfonso and Pedro represent two differentiated — though not opposed — strategies of livelihood within the same household economy. The rebusque is not available to all; it requires capital, contacts, knowledge, and a tolerance for mobility that not all residents possess equally.

III. Rebusque — Concept

The rebusque — from the verb rebuscar, to rummage or forage — describes an informal economy practice of buying low and selling higher through petty commerce.23 For Don Alfonso, it carried a sense of dignity and self-sufficiency distinct from wage labour or welfare dependence.24 It was, in short, a mark of self-reliance, not a last resort.
